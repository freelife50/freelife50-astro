<h2 id="why-bluetooth-doesnt-work-underwater" class="wp-block-heading">Why Bluetooth Earbuds Don't Work Underwater</h2>
<figure class="wp-block-image size-large"><img decoding="async" src="/images/wp-content/uploads/2026/07/swim-earphones-bluetooth.jpg" alt="A smartphone left on the poolside while a swimmer glides underwater, illustrating how the radio signal cuts off at the surface" /></figure>
<p>Before we get to the upgrade story, here's the single most important thing.</p>
<p>You might think: "Can't I just put my wireless earbuds in a waterproof case and leave my phone on the poolside?" <strong>Physically impossible</strong>, I'm afraid.</p>
<p>Bluetooth uses the 2.4GHz band, and that frequency is <strong>absorbed by water extremely fast</strong>. A signal that travels 30 feet on land <strong>dies within a couple of inches underwater</strong>. The moment your head goes under, the music stops. It's not a waterproofing problem — it's physics.</p>
<p>That's why every swimming headphone on the market works the same way: <strong>you store music files (MP3, etc.) on the device itself and play them standalone</strong>. You connect it to your computer and transfer your playlist. For a guy who grew up with a Walkman, it's actually nostalgic.</p>
<p>Most current models are <strong>dual-mode: Bluetooth on land, built-in memory playback in the water</strong>. Mine is this type, with 32GB of storage — up to 8,000 songs. I don't think the day I need 8,000 songs will ever come.</p>

<h2 id="do-public-pools-in-japan-allow-headphones" class="wp-block-heading">Do Public Pools in Japan Allow Headphones?</h2>
<figure class="wp-block-image size-large"><img decoding="async" src="/images/wp-content/uploads/2026/07/swim-earphones-pool.jpg" alt="A bright indoor public swimming pool with quiet lap lanes divided by lane ropes" /></figure>
<p>One thing to check before you buy.</p>
<p>Japanese public and municipal pools have traditionally banned <strong>"watches, jewelry, wearables, and all electronic devices"</strong> outright — due to collision injuries, breakage hazards, and camera-privacy concerns.</p>
<p>But the tide is turning. More pools now allow smartwatches with protective covers, and for headphones, a growing number of facilities have adopted this rule: <strong>"In-ear (canal-type) earphones are not allowed, but bone-conduction headphones that leave your ears open are permitted."</strong> Some public pools in Tokyo now explicitly allow waterproof bone-conduction headphones only.</p>
<p>The reason is the design itself. With your ear canals open, <strong>you can still hear the lifeguard's whistle and other swimmers approaching</strong>. That satisfies the safety requirements.</p>
<p>That said, <strong>rules genuinely vary from pool to pool</strong>. Check the posted rules or the facility's website, and give the lifeguard a quick heads-up before you swim. If you're at a base pool, the rules will be different again — a quick question to the staff saves the disappointment of new gear you can't use.</p>

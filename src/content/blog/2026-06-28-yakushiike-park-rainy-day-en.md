<p><strong>Reading time:</strong> about 9 minutes</p>

<p>Hello, this is Hiroshi from "Ramen, Beer, and Sometimes a Blog."</p>
<p>The other day, on a rainy weekday, I went to <strong>Yakushiike Park (Yakushi-ike)</strong> in Machida, Tokyo. Its full name is "Machida Yakushiike Park, Shikisai no Mori," and the pond is its centerpiece.</p>
<p>I'll be honest: my main goal that day wasn't a walk. I run a small BGM (background-music) video channel on the side, and I went to <strong>film footage of the rain and of the old water wheel</strong>. That's why my Shiba Inu, Momiji, stayed home this time — I didn't want her getting soaked, and I didn't yet have a dog raincoat. (I fixed that as soon as I got home. More on that at the end.)</p>
<p>Walking around with an umbrella in one hand, the thing that struck me was this: <strong>a rainy day at Yakushiike is a real hidden gem.</strong> In this post I'll focus on what people actually want to know before visiting: <strong>is it worth going in the rain, when do the hydrangeas and lotus peak, and the parking / hours / time needed / dog rules.</strong></p>

<p><strong>Who this is for</strong></p>
<ul class="wp-block-list">
<li>Anyone wondering whether to visit Yakushiike Park in the rain or during the rainy season</li>
<li>People who want to know when the hydrangeas and Ohga lotus peak, and how crowded it gets</li>
<li>Anyone needing parking fees, opening hours, and whether dogs are allowed</li>
</ul>

<p><strong>The short answers</strong></p>
<ul class="wp-block-list">
<li><strong>A rainy day is actually the best time.</strong> On a rainy weekday I practically had the pond to myself. The air was cool, and — bliss — no mosquitoes. The water was mirror-still and the photos came out beautifully.</li>
<li><strong>Hydrangeas peak in late June</strong> — white "Annabelle" blooms mixed with blue and purple, gorgeous in the rain. The <strong>Ohga lotus</strong> (an ancient variety) is just starting; its peak is from <strong>mid-July</strong> (the early-morning "lotus viewing" event is in July too). In late June you'll mostly see buds and big leaves, with the odd early bloom.</li>
<li><strong>Free admission. Parking holds 457 cars (first hour free, max ¥800/day). Open 6:00–18:00 (until 19:00 in June–August).</strong> Plan about 1–1.5 hours for a loop plus the flowers. <strong>Dogs are allowed on the walking paths on a leash</strong>, but some areas (the lawn, the wildflower garden) are off-limits to pets.</li>
</ul>

<h2 id="a-rainy-day-is-actually-the-best-time" class="wp-block-heading">A rainy day is actually the best time | quiet, cool, no mosquitoes</h2>
<figure class="wp-block-image size-large"><img decoding="async" src="/images/wp-content/uploads/2026/06/yakushiike-park-rainy-day-pond-2.jpg" alt="Yakushiike Park on a rainy day, the still pond reflecting green trees and an arched bridge" /></figure>
<p>I parked at the West Garden (Nishien) as usual and headed for the pond with an umbrella. Within a minute I thought, "I picked a good day."</p>
<p>First, <strong>almost nobody was there.</strong> A weekday, in the rain — not many people decide to visit Yakushiike on a day like that. So I had the whole loop to myself. No waiting for people to clear the frame, no strangers wandering into shots. The <strong>still water mirrored the forest and the arched bridge</strong> — it looked like an ink-wash painting.</p>
<p>Second, it was <strong>cool.</strong> I'd braced for the muggy heat you expect in late June, but the rain kept the temperature down, so I wasn't drenched in sweat. Best of all, <strong>the mosquitoes left me alone.</strong> They don't seem to fly in the rain — a huge win for someone like me who hates them.</p>
<p>You might think "a park in the rain? no thanks," but <strong>Yakushiike during the rainy season has a stillness and a sense of having the place to yourself that you never get on a sunny day.</strong> Hydrangeas and lotus are flowers that come alive in the wet. With an umbrella, a rainy day is honestly the smart choice.</p>

<h2 id="hydrangeas-peak-in-late-june" class="wp-block-heading">Hydrangeas peak in late June | white Annabelle and shades of blue</h2>
<figure class="wp-block-image size-large"><img decoding="async" src="/images/wp-content/uploads/2026/06/yakushiike-park-rainy-day-hydrangea-3.jpg" alt="Rain-soaked lacecap hydrangeas at Yakushiike Park, pale pink and blue petals dotted with water" /></figure>
<p>The thing that most made the trip worth it was the <strong>hydrangeas.</strong> Late June is right in the sweet spot.</p>
<p>Round white <strong>Annabelle</strong> blooms cluster together, with <strong>blue and purple hydrangeas</strong> woven in between. Petals holding raindrops glow with deeper color than they ever do in the sun. The pale pink-purple lacecaps looked almost translucent in the wet. Hydrangeas really are "rain flowers."</p>
<p>They aren't packed into one big "hydrangea garden" here — instead they're scattered around the pond and near the water wheel, so you <strong>keep coming across them as you walk.</strong> Turning each corner in the rain to find a new color is part of the charm of this park.</p>
<p>If you like the "rain + hydrangeas" combination, I also wrote about <a href="/sagamihara-azamizo-park-hydrangea-walk-en/">the hydrangea walk at Azamizo Park in Sagamihara</a>.</p>

<h2 id="the-ohga-lotus-peaks-from-mid-july" class="wp-block-heading">The Ohga lotus peaks from mid-July | late June is buds and early blooms</h2>
<figure class="wp-block-image size-large"><img decoding="async" src="/images/wp-content/uploads/2026/06/yakushiike-park-rainy-day-lotus-2.jpg" alt="A single pink Ohga lotus blooming in the rain in the lotus paddy at Yakushiike Park" /></figure>
<p>The other star of Yakushiike is the <strong>Ohga lotus</strong> — an "ancient lotus" grown from seeds found in a roughly 2,000-year-old layer of soil. The lotus paddy here is a real local highlight.</p>
<p>But let me be straight with you: <strong>in late June it was only just starting.</strong> The paddy was full of big green leaves, but only a few flowers. One early bloom stood in the rain, and it was lovely — but if you come expecting a sea of lotus flowers, this is a touch early.</p>
<figure class="wp-block-image size-large"><img decoding="async" src="/images/wp-content/uploads/2026/06/yakushiike-park-rainy-day-lotus-leaf-2.jpg" alt="Rainwater pooled into a bead at the center of a large lotus leaf at Yakushiike Park" /></figure>
<p><strong>The Ohga lotus peaks from mid-July.</strong> The early-morning "lotus viewing" event is held in July, with the park opening at 7:00 a.m. Lotus flowers open in the morning and close by midday, so <strong>to catch the blooms, aim for early morning from mid-July onward.</strong></p>
<p>That said, <strong>the lotus paddy is worth seeing in the rain even without flowers.</strong> Rainwater gathers into a single bead in the middle of each giant leaf, rolling like mercury when the wind shifts. That's a sight you only get on a wet day.</p>

<h2 id="the-water-wheel-and-the-yakuimon-gate" class="wp-block-heading">The water wheel and the Yakuimon gate | rain-soaked countryside</h2>
<figure class="wp-block-image size-large"><img decoding="async" src="/images/wp-content/uploads/2026/06/yakushiike-park-rainy-day-waterwheel-2.jpg" alt="A thatched water-wheel hut surrounded by green at Yakushiike Park, moss on the rain-soaked roof" /></figure>
<p>One of my filming targets that day was this <strong>water wheel.</strong> A small thatched hut tucked into the greenery, moss on the rain-darkened roof — it had real character. The creak of the turning wheel mixed with the sound of the rain made a stillness I could have stood in forever. Pure Japanese countryside, perfect for a BGM video.</p>
<p>Around the pond, Yakushiike also has the <strong>Yakuimon gate</strong> and old farmhouses (the former Nagai and Ogino residences). It's less a plain park than <strong>a preserved slice of pre-modern rural Japan</strong> — stone-paved paths, a teahouse, mixed woodland. The old wood and stone take on a quiet, settled color in the rain, and just walking calms you down.</p>
<p>The name "Yakushi-ike" comes from the neighboring <strong>Nozuta Yakushido temple hall.</strong> The park opened in 1976 and is designated a Scenic Spot of Tokyo. History, countryside, and flowers all in one place — a generous little park.</p>

<p><strong>Parking tip:</strong> entrances are split between North, East, and West. For the hydrangeas and pond, the East lots are closest; for the squirrel garden, plum grove, and West-Garden course, use the West lot. <strong>Rainy days and weekdays are rarely crowded,</strong> but weekends during the plum, hydrangea, and autumn-foliage seasons fill up, so take public transit then.</p>
<p><strong>About dogs:</strong> I left Momiji home this time, so she doesn't appear in these photos. But from past family visits, <strong>you can walk a dog on the paths on a leash.</strong> The lawn and the wildflower garden are off-limits to pets, so watch for the signs. <h2 id="one-last-thing-the-dog-raincoat" class="wp-block-heading">One last thing | the dog raincoat</h2>
<p>As I said at the start, I left Momiji home because I didn't want her getting soaked. But as I walked, I kept thinking, "I wish I'd shown her this rainy Yakushiike."</p>
<p>The still water, the rain-soaked hydrangeas, the empty paths — this is exactly the kind of place a dog would love. So the moment I got home, I ordered a <strong>dog raincoat.</strong> Now the next rainy day, Momiji can come too. I hope to write "Momiji and a rainy Yakushiike" next time.</p>

<h2 id="summary" class="wp-block-heading">Summary</h2>
<p>A rainy day at Yakushiike Park means few people, cool air, and no mosquitoes — a rainy-season hideaway where you can have the still water and the hydrangeas to yourself.</p>
<ul class="wp-block-list">
<li><strong>A rainy day is the smart choice</strong> — on a rainy weekday you'll have the pond to yourself, cool and mosquito-free.</li>
<li><strong>Hydrangeas peak in late June; the Ohga lotus from mid-July</strong> (for the blooms, aim for a July morning).</li>

<p>Grab an umbrella and take a slow walk around a rainy Yakushiike. You'll find a quieter side of Machida than any sunny day will show you.</p>
